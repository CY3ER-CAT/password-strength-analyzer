04 Field notes

The thinking behind the score

A quick, honest primer on what actually protects a password — and what the numbers can and cannot tell you.

01

What makes a password secure?

Three things matter most: enough length, genuine unpredictability, and exclusivity. Complexity helps, but a long, random password beats a short, clever one. The practical answer is to let a password manager create and remember a unique secret for every account.

02

What is password entropy?

Entropy measures uncertainty in bits. Every extra bit doubles the number of guesses an attacker needs. The simple estimate below assumes a password is generated randomly — human-chosen substitutions and words collapse that number dramatically.

E = L × log2(N)
03

Why are longer passwords better?

Each added character multiplies the search space. Going from 8 to 12 characters is not a 50% improvement — it can mean billions of times more guessing work. A four-to-six-word passphrase can reach that safety margin while staying memorable.

04

Why should passwords be unique?

Breaches happen at the server, not at your keyboard. When one site leaks, attackers replay that email-and-password pair across banking, email, and social accounts — an attack called credential stuffing. Reuse turns a single breach into many compromised accounts.

05

What is brute-force cracking?

Brute force tries every possible combination. A modern GPU can test billions of candidates per second offline, and dictionary rules try common words, names, and substitutions first. That is why length and true randomness matter more than clever-looking character swaps.

06

Why should you enable MFA?

Multi-factor authentication adds a second proof — an app code, passkey, or security key — so a leaked password alone is not enough. Prefer phishing-resistant methods such as passkeys or hardware keys; SMS codes help, but are weaker. Save your recovery codes somewhere safe.

An estimate, not a guarantee. The score and crack-time figures are educational approximations. Crack time assumes an offline attacker testing 10 billion guesses per second against a randomly composed password; real attacks also exploit reuse, phishing, leaks, and weak server practices. A perfect score cannot protect an account that is reused or phished.
